# Characters and relationships

**Status:** Canonical direction; exact cast writing and numerical tuning remain open
**Last reviewed:** 2026-08-02

## Core rule

> Relationships are histories of shared work and choices, not friendship meters filled with gifts.

Leaf & Ember is about building a house with people, not acquiring workers and contacts as upgrades. Named characters remember how the player works, teaches, negotiates, keeps promises, handles failure, and treats others when the house is under pressure.

The system must support professional respect without personal warmth, affection without commercial confidence, disagreement between close friends, and trust that has been damaged but meaningfully repaired.

## Intended emotional scope

The game supports:

- Close and enduring friendship
- Mentorship in either direction
- Chosen-family and family-like bonds
- Loyalty grounded in shared history
- Collegial affection, rivalry, disappointment, reconciliation, and grief
- Relationships that change as people gain skill, responsibility, and independence

Romance, dating, marriage, and romanceable-character routes are outside the intended scope. Emotional intimacy does not need to culminate in romance to be important.

Gifts may appear when a particular moment or character makes them meaningful, but there is no repeatable preferred-gift economy. Closeness grows primarily through time, attention, shared experiences, honest communication, and consequential conduct.

## Relationship state

No single friendship or loyalty value represents a relationship. The core dimensions are:

| Dimension | What it represents |
| --- | --- |
| Familiarity | How much history and mutual context the characters share |
| Trust | Confidence in honesty, discretion, care, and kept commitments |
| Respect | Regard for judgment, competence, principles, and demonstrated conduct |
| Warmth | Personal ease, affection, and willingness to be emotionally open |
| Strain | Active hurt, frustration, fear, resentment, or unresolved tension |

These dimensions are related but not interchangeable. A buyer may respect the player's palate without trusting their delivery estimates. An apprentice may feel deep warmth while losing confidence in the player's leadership. A rival may trust the player to negotiate honestly while disliking their methods.

Relationship state is directional and contextual. What one person feels or believes need not be symmetrical, and the game does not assign feelings to the player. Only authored relationships relevant to play require detailed simulation; the system does not need to maintain a fully connected social graph.

## Role-specific confidence

Personal relationship state is supplemented by evidence specific to a role.

### Employment and workshop life

Employees and artisans consider:

- Confidence that pay will be fair and timely
- Workload, fatigue, safety, and working conditions
- Recognition and credit for their work
- Access to learning and meaningful responsibility
- Creative autonomy within house standards
- Confidence in the player's leadership and decisions

### Trade and commercial work

Growers, suppliers, buyers, and commercial partners consider:

- Payment and delivery reliability
- Fairness and clarity in negotiation
- Contract and communication history
- Respect for provenance and attribution
- Confidence in the house's quality and consistency
- Competition, confidentiality, and exclusivity commitments

### Teaching and mentorship

Teachers and learners consider:

- Compatibility of teaching and learning methods
- Willingness to receive correction
- Confidence with the work
- Progress toward independent judgment
- Methodological disagreements
- Readiness for greater responsibility

A strong personal bond does not grant magical skill, better prices, or unconditional compliance. It creates openness, patience, candor, and opportunities where those responses make sense.

## Memory and knowledge

Characters remember consequential events, including:

- Promises made, kept, renegotiated, or broken
- Pay, workload, safety, and working conditions
- Teaching, correction, responsibility, and earned independence
- Public or private credit for an idea or piece of work
- Support during a bad crop, failed release, illness, or financial crisis
- Fair and unfair negotiations
- Successful collaborations and shared experiments
- Mistakes that were admitted, concealed, blamed on someone else, or repaired
- Contracts that failed and what the player did afterward

A memory records who was involved, what the character believes occurred, how they learned it, its importance, when it happened, and whether it remains unresolved. Characters know only what they observe, are told, or credibly hear through their social and professional networks. Their understanding can be partial or mistaken.

The game should not preserve every greeting or routine interaction forever. Minor experiences can accumulate into a qualitative impression; defining events remain available as specific memories. The social simulation must be legible enough that important reactions have understandable causes.

## Commitments

A commitment is explicit social state rather than flavor text. It records:

- The people or organizations involved
- What was promised
- The expected date, window, or condition
- Whether it is private, professional, or public
- Its current status
- Any renegotiation and the reason for it
- How it was fulfilled, missed, released, or repaired

Renegotiating early and honestly differs from silently failing. A missed promise does not apply one universal penalty: its effect depends on the stakes, explanation, prior history, and consequences for the other person.

## How relationships grow

Meaningful relationship play comes from:

- Working beside someone and learning how they approach the craft
- Keeping agreements when doing so is inconvenient
- Teaching patiently or accepting another person's expertise
- Sharing credit and acknowledging authorship
- Negotiating fairly without pretending interests never conflict
- Supporting another person's goals, not merely extracting their usefulness
- Admitting uncertainty or mistakes before they become someone else's problem
- Taking responsibility during difficult crops, releases, or financial periods
- Spending authored social time together away from immediate transactions

Routine conversation can maintain texture, but there is no daily social-maintenance tax and no optimal gift schedule.

## Character identity and agency

Every major character has an authored identity containing:

- Work, community role, and relevant history
- Values, boundaries, methods, and points of pride
- Capabilities and areas of uncertainty
- Current needs and long-term ambitions
- Relationships to other people and institutions
- Subjects they disclose only with sufficient context or trust
- Conditions under which they collaborate, refuse, confront, forgive, or leave

Dynamic goals let characters respond to the changing house without requiring an unrestricted life simulator. A character may seek mastery, stable work, recognition, independence, security for others, a better market for their crop, or proof that a method deserves respect.

Characters do not exist solely to reward the player. They can decline an offer, defend a colleague, challenge a decision, pursue another opportunity, leave employment, or eventually become a peer, supplier, mentor, or competitor.

## Named artisans

### Recruitment

Artisans enter the player's orbit through the local community, recommendations, prior work, teaching relationships, and the house's reputation. Recruitment is a mutual decision. Pay, role, schedule, expectations, and learning opportunities should be understood before someone joins the house.

### Capability

An artisan can develop distinct tendencies across:

- Leaf handling, preparation, and sorting
- Bunching and draw control
- Wrapper selection and application
- Particular vitolas, methods, or leaf conditions
- Precision, consistency, and working pace
- Sensory evaluation and fault diagnosis
- Fermentation, aging, and material stewardship
- Teaching, coordination, and workshop leadership

These are developing capabilities, not fixed character classes. The same underlying material and construction simulation governs player and artisan work; delegation does not replace the craft system with an arbitrary worker bonus.

### Learning and career

Artisans learn through practice, observation, feedback, deliberate teaching, experiments, and increasing responsibility. Career descriptions may include helper, apprentice, developing artisan, independent artisan, specialist, supervisor, master, and mentor, but they are not a mandatory linear ladder.

Someone may become an extraordinary specialist without wanting to supervise. Another may value stable, respected work more than public acclaim. A talented apprentice may eventually want an independent venture. Supporting that ambition can become part of the player's legacy rather than being treated only as a loss.

### Employment and departure

People can become dissatisfied or leave, but departure must have visible causes and warning states. Relevant causes include:

- Unreliable or unfair pay
- Unsafe or persistently excessive workload
- Repeated lack of credit or advancement
- Unresolved personal or methodological conflict
- Loss of confidence in the player's leadership
- A compelling outside opportunity
- A stated goal of independence

The player should have opportunities to listen, respond, renegotiate, or prepare a respectful transition. A departure does not erase the character from the world; former employees can remain friends, collaborators, suppliers, rivals, or respected peers.

## Relationship patterns by role

| Role | What matters especially | Possible relationship outcomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grower | Fair crop treatment, timely payment, provenance, shared risk, agricultural respect | Preferred access, experiments, advance agreements, candid crop information |
| Artisan or employee | Pay, conditions, credit, teaching, autonomy, leadership | Mastery, stewardship, mentorship, conflict, departure, independent collaboration |
| Buyer or exporter | Delivery, candor, audience fit, consistency, contract conduct | Better information, larger trust-based commitments, introductions, renegotiation flexibility |
| Critic or evaluator | Access without coercion, demonstrated intent, honesty, independence | Deeper dialogue and contextual feedback, not guaranteed praise |
| Competitor or peer | Fair competition, method, attribution, boundaries, mutual capability | Rivalry, exchange, collaboration, respect, resentment, or alliance |
| Craft or community contact | Reliability, reciprocity, participation, effects on local people | Better collaboration, social support, shared events, or community opposition |

Access follows credible relationship history. A friend can still reject bad business, a critic never owes a favorable review, and a grower with finite leaf may choose another buyer.

## Conflict and repair

Conflict should emerge from recognizable interests and conduct rather than random drama. Common sources include quality versus deadlines, workload versus ambition, tradition versus experiment, personal authorship versus house identity, scarce leaf, contract obligations, and mistakes under pressure.

Dialogue can acknowledge harm, but repair requires relevant action. Depending on the conflict, the player may need to pay what is owed, reduce workload, restore credit, disclose a mistake, revise a contract, replace damaged material, support a goal, or demonstrate changed behavior over time.

One dialogue choice should not permanently remove essential gameplay. Serious choices can close a particular relationship or opportunity, but alternate people and paths must keep the game playable. Reconciliation is possible where it fits the character; it is not guaranteed or instant.

## Authored and systemic content

Leaf & Ember uses a hybrid structure.

### Authored content

Authored scenes carry introductions, personal histories, major career decisions, mentorship milestones, conflicts, reconciliations, departures, celebrations, and defining choices. These scenes give each character a particular voice and prevent relationships from feeling interchangeable.

### Systemic content

Systemic interactions acknowledge work in progress, schedules, recent memories, commitments, conditions at the finca, business events, and repeated collaboration. They provide continuity between authored scenes without attempting unconstrained generated conversation.

Major scenes can be triggered by combinations of calendar state, memories, relationship qualities, goals, employment state, and house events. The system should select an appropriate authored response or event; it does not need to simulate every thought or generate unlimited dialogue.

## Dialogue and player expression

Choices express priorities and conduct rather than a simple kind-versus-cruel morality scale. The player may have to decide whether to:

- Protect quality or honor a delivery date
- Ask for overtime or reduce the release
- Admit a hidden mistake or risk discovery
- Share authorship or claim a house achievement personally
- Accept criticism, defend a method, or propose another test
- Hold someone to an agreement or release them from it
- Support an artisan's independence or try to retain them

Several choices can be defensible while revealing different values. Tone matters, but later conduct determines whether words become trusted evidence.

## Schedules and access

Characters need believable work and personal rhythms without becoming a search-and-wait puzzle.

- The journal shows known availability and upcoming appointments.
- Meetings can be arranged through messages, visits, or staff rather than requiring the player to locate someone at a precise minute.
- Important visitors and deadlines appear in advance on the calendar.
- Missed ordinary encounters can recur or be rescheduled.
- Urgent character events clearly communicate their window and stakes.

Physical presence should make the finca and city feel inhabited. It should not punish the player for failing to memorize hidden schedules.

## Player-facing information

The character journal presents observed and disclosed information, not raw relationship numbers. It can show:

- A qualitative summary such as 'respects your fermentation judgment but doubts your delivery planning'
- Known role, goals, methods, and preferences
- Active commitments and recent shared history
- Employment terms, workload concerns, and development goals where appropriate
- Known availability and planned meetings
- Important unresolved disagreements

The interface must distinguish what the player knows from hidden character state. New evidence can correct an earlier impression.

## Initial ensemble

The first substantial playable should build a small, interconnected ensemble around these roles:

- A neighboring grower whose finite lots and crop risks establish sourcing as a human relationship
- A prospective apprentice or developing artisan who makes teaching and employment personal
- An experienced, opinionated craftsperson who can advise, disagree, and evaluate the player's standards
- A buyer or export representative willing to consider the inaugural release
- A box maker, printer, or packaging craftsperson who connects presentation to local work
- A competing proprietor or blender whose methods create pressure without requiring villainy

Each is a named, authored person rather than a generic vendor. Some should already know one another so choices travel through a credible community. Final names, biographies, language, and cultural detail require the focused setting research defined in [`OPEN_QUESTIONS.md`](OPEN_QUESTIONS.md).

Close friendship, mentorship, and family-like bonds can emerge from this ensemble over years of play. No character is designed as a romance route.

## Conceptual entities

Implementation should preserve distinct state for:

- Character definition and dynamic character state
- Directed relationship state
- Role-specific confidence or concern
- Memory and accumulated impression
- Commitment
- Character goal and boundary
- Skill profile and learning history
- Employment agreement and work state
- Schedule, appointment, and availability
- Authored scene and systemic event trigger

Exact field layouts and formulas remain implementation work. These concepts must not be collapsed into one relationship score or one generic NPC level.

## Vertical-slice requirements

The character portion of the vertical slice should prove that the player can:

1. Meet at least three named people with distinct roles, goals, and methods.
2. Make and fulfill or renegotiate a meaningful commitment.
3. Work or taste beside someone and receive character-specific feedback.
4. Teach, learn from, or share responsibility with a developing artisan.
5. Experience one disagreement whose repair depends on an appropriate action.
6. See professional confidence and personal warmth communicate different information.
7. Arrange an interaction without waiting at an unknown location.
8. Read a qualitative history in the character journal without seeing relationship numbers.

## Design constraints

- Never reduce a person to a production multiplier, vendor menu, or reward track.
- Never use one friendship, loyalty, or approval score as the entire relationship.
- Never require repetitive gifts or daily greetings to preserve important bonds.
- Never let affection erase professional interests, boundaries, or consequences.
- Never treat employees as property or departures as random punishment.
- Never make romance the expected reward for emotional intimacy; romance is outside scope.
- Make major reactions traceable to known history, goals, and present circumstances.
- Keep the cast small enough for recurring work, change, and authored specificity.
- Preserve alternate gameplay paths when a particular relationship ends.
- Let years of shared craft turn colleagues, teachers, students, and friends into part of the house's legacy.
