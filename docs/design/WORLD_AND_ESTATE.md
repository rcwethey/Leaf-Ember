# World and estate

**Status:** Canonical direction
**Last reviewed:** 2026-08-02

## Premise

The player acquires a neglected small finca and integrated cigar workshop outside a nearby tobacco-industry city. The property has usable land, a few aging spaces, and buildings with history, but it does not give the player a famous family name or predetermined legacy.

The opening is about making the place viable and earning the first honest response to a cigar bearing the player's chosen house name.

## Setting

The game takes place in a fictional highland valley and nearby city inspired primarily by the geography, tobacco economy, architecture, and culture of northern Nicaragua.

Using one primary reference culture is important. The setting must not treat Central America as a single interchangeable aesthetic. Final names, history, language use, and character backgrounds require focused cultural research and, when production permits, review by people familiar with the region.

The world is contemporary enough to support modern export markets and equipment, but the exact year remains open until its gameplay consequences are understood.

## World structure

Leaf & Ember uses intimate, authored locations rather than one enormous map.

### The finca

The finca is the player's home, workplace, principal simulation space, and emotional center. It contains the full transformation from living plant to finished cigar.

The long-term estate may include:

- Seedbeds or a modest nursery
- Distinct field plots
- Curing barns, or *casas de tabaco*
- Leaf receiving and sorting space
- Fermentation rooms with working *pilones*
- Conditioning and preparation rooms
- The rolling workshop
- Quality-control and developmental tasting space
- Aging rooms
- Packaging and shipping
- Office, records, and living quarters
- A courtyard or other natural gathering place

Facilities should be physically legible. A leaf lot moves through the estate, changes at each stage, and retains the history of where it has been and who handled it.

### The nearby city

The city is the commercial and institutional center rather than a second farm. It can provide:

- Leaf brokers, exporters, and buyers
- Banks and business services
- Printers, box makers, and other craftspeople
- Markets, cafés, hotels, and meeting places
- Established factories and competing houses
- Agricultural and regulatory services
- Festivals, forums, exhibitions, and major trade events

The city should feel connected to the surrounding growing valleys. It is where agricultural work becomes industry, reputation, and access to the wider world.

### Other farms

Other growers are not anonymous resource nodes. Visiting their farms reveals different land, practices, constraints, and specialties. Relationships with them provide leaf, knowledge, contracts, and opportunities the player's finca cannot produce alone.

### The wider market

The wider world is expressed selectively through visiting buyers, correspondence, orders, reviews, exhibitions, export relationships, and occasional travel. The game does not need to construct every destination as an open world in order to make the house's reach feel international.

## Community

Characters are named people whose work places them naturally inside the cigar economy and local community. Important roles may include:

- Growers and farm workers
- Leaf sorters and fermentation specialists
- Rollers and workshop supervisors
- Agricultural advisers
- Box makers, printers, and designers
- Merchants, exporters, and visiting buyers
- Critics, collectors, and private clients
- Proprietors and artisans from rival houses
- Neighbors and community figures not defined solely by tobacco

Employees must not be presented as a count of generic labor. Their experience, specialties, relationships, working conditions, and aspirations are part of the house the player is building.

## Social and commercial spaces

A conventional consumer cigar lounge is not the default center of local life. Feedback and relationships can emerge through:

- Developmental tastings at the workshop table
- Meals and conversations in the finca courtyard
- Buyer and grower visits
- Factory and farm tours
- Markets, cafés, hotels, and professional meetings
- Seasonal festivals and industry gatherings
- Reports from retailers and exporters abroad
- Private commissions and hosted estate events

A visitor-facing tasting room may become a later estate option, but it should grow from the house's identity rather than being assumed at the start.

## Tone and sensory direction

The world is grounded, warm, mature, and observant. Atmosphere should come from material and work: rain on a curing-barn roof, changing light across the fields, the scent of a fermentation room, tobacco bundles being opened, a chaveta against wood, quiet conversation over coffee, and the wait before someone responds to a new blend.

Humor, celebration, rivalry, and conflict belong in the world, but the baseline is not cartoonish frenzy. The game respects the labor and cultural knowledge behind its subject.

## Opening experience

The intended opening arc is:

1. Inspect the neglected finca and workshop.
2. Restore the minimum spaces required to handle leaf safely.
3. Meet a local grower and acquire a small complementary leaf selection.
4. Evaluate the available tobacco and create a first prototype.
5. Identify and correct a blend or construction problem.
6. Produce a tiny inaugural batch.
7. Convince a buyer to take a chance on it.
8. Receive the first genuine response to the player's work.

The first box sold should matter more emotionally than a late-game production statistic.
